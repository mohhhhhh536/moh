# Product Evaluation — 10% Azelaic Acid + Niacinamide Redness Serum

**Store:** Thessvane · **Composite: 7.9/10 (63/80)** · **Verdict: TEST, conditionally**

A fragrance-free leave-on serum for persistent facial redness and rosacea-prone skin.
Strong problem, strong customer, weak on the two things paid social actually rewards:
instant visual payoff and impulse pricing.

## The product, specifically

| | |
|---|---|
| Format | 30ml serum, airless pump |
| Actives | 10% azelaic acid, 4% niacinamide |
| Excludes | Fragrance, essential oils, alcohol, menthol |
| Concern | Facial redness, post-acne marks |
| Landed COGS | $5–7 at 1,000 units |
| Anchor price | $42 single unit |

## Scorecard

Every score carries its deduction. A 9 with no stated weakness is a number I made up.

### Solves a clear problem — 9/10
Redness is visible, persistent, and self-diagnosed — she doesn't need an ad to tell her she
has it. Makeup covers it and never fixes it, which is exactly the gap a product can fill.
**Minus one:** it's management, not cure. Rosacea is chronic, and heat, wine or a bad week
will beat your serum on any given Thursday.

### Emotional buying potential — 9/10
Redness reads back to her as looking angry, drunk, or sunburned. It's the one skin concern
people apologise for out loud. High shame, high avoidance — skipped photos, ten extra minutes
of colour-correcting every morning. **Minus one:** shame-driven categories are quiet. Expect a
low review and UGC submission rate, which starves your social proof exactly when you need it.

### Existing consumer demand — 8/10
Real, large, and growing — azelaic went mainstream and the search intent is already there.
But demand that already exists is demand someone already serves. The Ordinary, Paula's Choice
and Naturium all ship a 10%. You are not creating a market, you are trying to intercept one at
three times the cheapest price in it.

### Profit margin potential — 8/10
$5–7 landed against $42 is ~85% gross, and at under 150g shipping barely touches it.
**Minus two, and it matters:** gross margin is not contribution margin. Private label means
real cash upfront at MOQ, and the $12 shelf anchor caps how far you can push price no matter
how good the formula is.

### Ability to demonstrate visually in ads — 6/10
**The weakest link, and the one most people score wrong.** Redness does photograph well and
moves faster than most concerns. But: Meta restricts before/after skin imagery, so your best
creative is your biggest account risk; red skin on camera is lighting-dependent, so honest
results look faked; and the product is a clear serum with no texture moment, no gunk, no
satisfying reveal. You are filming an absence.

### Number of potential marketing angles — 8/10
Genuinely distinct angles, not five rewordings of one — label-led, price-objection-led,
education-led, honesty-led. **Minus two:** the two highest-converting ones ("clears rosacea",
"clears acne") are drug claims for a cosmetic. Those get creative rejected and, at scale,
invite worse than rejection.

### Ability to identify a specific target customer — 9/10
Unusually sharp and self-identifying. She already owns the vocabulary — "reactive",
"rosacea-prone", "fragrance-free" — and she uses it in search. **Minus one:** you cannot target
it on Meta. Health conditions were pulled as interest categories, so the creative has to do the
targeting the platform won't.

### Potential for impulse purchases — 6/10
$42 sits just above true impulse. Worse, the customer you want is the least impulsive buyer in
skincare — she has a shelf of half-used bottles that betrayed her and she now reads the full
INCI list before she buys. That's structural. No amount of urgency copy fixes a buyer who has
been burned six times.

## 1. The strongest reason someone would buy it

She isn't buying a serum. She's buying the ability to leave the house without a
colour-correcting layer under her foundation. That's the whole purchase.

The thing that actually closes her, though, is smaller and less romantic: fragrance-free, no
essential oils, no alcohol, no menthol. She has been promised "gentle" before and it stung.
The exclusion list is the proof; the outcome is the want.

## 2. The biggest problem with selling it

The Ordinary sells the same active at the same strength for about $12, and your customer is
the most ingredient-literate buyer in the category — she will flip your label and find it.

So you can't win on the molecule. You have to win on the vehicle — a real emulsion that layers
under makeup versus a gritty suspension that pills — and that is a very hard thing to prove in
fifteen seconds of video.

Stack it with the two low scores and you get the honest shape of this offer: your best creative
is policy-restricted, your best price is anchored by a $12 competitor, and your best customer
doesn't impulse-buy. Three structural headwinds on one SKU. None is fatal alone. Together they
mean this only works as a considered-purchase brand, never as a quick creative-and-flip.

## 3. Top 5 customer pain points

1. **Makeup covers it, nothing fixes it.** Persistent flush across cheeks and nose. She has a
   colour-correcting routine that costs her ten minutes every single morning and washes off
   every single night.
2. **Everything "gentle" still stings.** A graveyard of half-used bottles that were marketed to
   her exactly like this one will be. She now distrusts the entire category, including you.
3. **The mark outlasts the pimple by months.** The spot healed in a week. The red evidence of it
   is still there in October.
4. **She can't predict it.** Fine Monday, flaring Thursday, no idea which of four possible causes
   did it — so she can't plan a face around an important day.
5. **Being told it's "just sensitive skin."** Dismissed socially, and gatekept clinically — the
   real fix sits behind a prescription and a three-month wait for a dermatologist.

## 4. Top 5 marketing angles

1. **The ingredient list test — lead with this.** Hero the back label, not the face. No
   fragrance, no essential oils, no alcohol, no menthol. Ad-safe, on-brand for Thessvane, and it
   speaks straight to the trust deficit. Critically, it's the only one of these five with no
   policy risk at all.
2. **Answer the $12 question out loud.** Same active, different vehicle. Gritty suspension that
   pills under makeup versus a serum that layers. Own the objection on your own terms instead of
   hoping she doesn't Google it. She will Google it.
3. **The makeup-removal reveal.** Film her taking makeup off at week three instead of shooting a
   clinical before/after. Same proof, far lower chance of a restriction, and it's the more honest
   emotional moment anyway.
4. **"Not a cure. A floor."** Anti-hype positioning: this won't stop a flare, it raises your
   baseline. Converts unusually well with burned buyers and quietly pre-empts a chunk of your
   refunds.
5. **Trigger education.** Heat, alcohol, hot showers, that one "clean" facial oil everyone
   recommends. Cheap top-of-funnel content that builds the retargeting pool the other four
   angles then close.

## 5. The most likely target customer

Woman, 27–42, metro US / UK / AU / CA, household income $60k+. Self-describes as
"rosacea-prone" or "reactive". Buys fragrance-free by default. Has either seen a dermatologist
or is actively avoiding the cost and the wait.

She owns eight to ten skincare products and has abandoned half of them. She follows
dermatologist creators, screenshots ingredient panels, and reads reviews looking specifically
for other people with her skin type. She researches for four days and then buys the one product
that survived the research. Plan the funnel for a four-day consideration window, not a
four-minute one.

## 6. Recommended selling price range

**$38–44 for a single 30ml.** Below $34 you're arguing on The Ordinary's turf and you'll lose.
Above $48 you're in derm-brand territory without the credentials to hold it.

| Offer | Price | Per unit | Gross margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 bottle (anchor) | $42 | $42 | ~85% |
| 2 bottles | $72 | $36 | ~83% |
| Subscribe, every 8 wks | $34 | $34 | ~82% |
| + barrier cream attach | $28 | $28 | ~80% |

**The number that decides this:** at $42 AOV and 85% gross you have roughly $34 to cover CAC and
fulfilment. Blended skincare CPA on Meta realistically lands $38–55. Single-unit checkout loses
money on the first order. You need AOV at $62–70, which means an attach on ~40% of orders — the
routine builder already in the theme is the obvious mechanism, not an afterthought.

## 7. Why it fits this store specifically

Thessvane currently positions on "clean, natural, gentle." That is not a positioning, it's an
adjective, and every brand in the category uses it. It's the weakest thing on the site.

Redness and reactive skin is the one place where that adjective converts into a specification.
To this customer, "gentle" isn't a vibe — it's the screening criterion she buys on. Picking this
product turns the store's most generic claim into its sharpest one. That's the real strategic
argument for it, and it's a better argument than anything in the product itself.

## Verdict — TEST, but only in one specific shape

There is a real offer here, and it's built on the customer, not the product. The molecule is a
commodity; the buyer is not. **If you can only bring the molecule, this is a skip.**

### Conditions — all three, or don't start

- **Own the formula.** A real emulsion with azelaic + niacinamide, verifiably fragrance-free.
  Your only defence against a $12 anchor is a product that is actually different.
- **Bundle and subscription live on day one.** Not "we'll add it later." The single-unit maths
  doesn't work, so the offer structure isn't optional.
- **Build creative on the label angle first.** If you lead with before/afters you will eventually
  lose the ad account, and losing the pixel costs more than the test.

### Kill criteria — decide these now

- 3-sec hold rate under 25% across three distinct concepts. The hook isn't landing and the
  product isn't the reason.
- CTR under 1.2% after $500 spend across three concepts.
- CPA over $55 at $42 AOV after $1,500 spend with no downward trend.
- Ad account restricted on skin imagery inside two weeks. The lane you need just closed —
  reassess before spending more.
- Refund or complaint rate over 8% at day 45. The formula is stinging the exact customer it was
  built for. Fatal. Stop.

### When this is a flat skip

If the plan is to dropship a generic white-label azelaic serum, skip it. The entire thesis rests
on the formula being demonstrably better than a $12 suspension, and there is no version of
dropshipping that gets you there.
